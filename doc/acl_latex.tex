\begin{abstract}
Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) exposes large language models to knowledge-poisoning attacks, where misinformation injected into retrieved documents can influence model outputs. Prior work has shown that models may detect contradictory evidence yet still allow it to influence their responses, revealing a gap between monitoring and control. We investigate whether deliberative reasoning changes this relationship. Attack Success Rate (ASR) and Poison Detection Rate (PDR) alone do not reveal whether detected poison continues to influence the final answer. Thus we use two complementary measures: Cordon Rate (CR), the probability that a model detects poison and nevertheless produces a poison-aligned answer, conditioned on a non-poison-aligned no-RAG response; and Leakage Rate (LR), the probability that poisoned context influences the answer despite an explicit instruction to ignore retrieved documents. Across 200 SciFact questions, we compare reasoning-disabled and reasoning-enabled configurations of DeepSeek-V4-Flash and Qwen3.6-Plus. For DeepSeek-V4-Flash, reasoning reduces CR from 0.205 to 0.075 and LR from 0.235 to 0.140, despite increasing ASR from 0.233 to 0.298 and decreasing PDR from 0.965 to 0.665. Qwen3.6-Plus shows the same qualitative pattern. These results demonstrate that poison detection and downstream influence capture distinct aspects of poisoning behavior.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The reliance of Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) on external knowledge to enhance large language models, may expose RAG systems to knowledge-poisoning attacks, in which adversarial documents can influence model responses. Prior work has shown that models may detect poisoned evidence while still allowing it to affect their final answers, revealing a gap between monitoring and control. For an example, see Appendix~\ref{app:example}. The \emph{Cordon Principle}~\cite{yu2026cordonmasdefendingragknowledge} addresses this vulnerability by preventing agents responsible for final answer synthesis from directly accessing raw evidence, but such isolation introduces architectural and computational constraints.

Rather than proposing a new defense mechanism, we empirically investigate whether deliberative, System 2-style reasoning~\cite{kahneman2011thinking,bengio2019system} changes the relationship between poison detection and downstream influence. A key challenge is that attack success and poison detection alone do not reveal whether detected poisoned evidence continues to affect the final answer. We therefore distinguish detection from downstream influence and evaluate them separately. We use \emph{Cordon Rate} (CR), which measures the probability that a model detects poisoned evidence and nevertheless produces a poison-aligned answer, conditioned on a non-poison-aligned no-RAG response, and \emph{Leakage Rate} (LR), which measures the probability that poisoned context influences the answer despite an explicit instruction to ignore retrieved documents. The two measures target different stages of poisoning behavior. Poison Detection Rate (PDR) asks whether the model recognizes the retrieved misinformation, whereas CR asks whether that recognized misinformation nevertheless affects the final answer. LR tests a different failure mode: whether poisoned context influences the answer even when the model is explicitly instructed to ignore the retrieved documents. Thus, detection, resistance after detection, and resistance to explicit context suppression are related but distinct properties. Under the expected behavior, detected or explicitly ignored poisoned context should not influence the final answer, yielding $CR=0$ and $LR=0$.

We evaluate reasoning-disabled and reasoning-enabled configurations of DeepSeek-V4-Flash and Qwen3.6-Plus on 200 randomly selected SciFact questions. Within-model comparisons show that reasoning reduces both CR and LR, even as Attack Success Rate (ASR) increases and PDR decreases. For DeepSeek-V4-Flash, CR falls from 0.205 to 0.075 and LR from 0.235 to 0.140, while ASR rises from 0.233 to 0.298 and PDR drops from 0.965 to 0.665; Qwen3.6-Plus shows the same pattern.

Our contributions are:

(1) an empirical evaluation of how deliberative reasoning affects the relationship between poison detection and downstream influence in RAG;

(2) evidence that reasoning can reduce the frequency with which detected poisoned context continues to influence the final answer, even as overall attack success increases and poison detection becomes less reliable; and

(3) two complementary measures, Cordon Rate and Leakage Rate, for evaluating downstream influence beyond attack success and poison detection.


\section{Approach}

\subsection{Motivation}
Prior work~\cite{yu2026detectingresolvingmonitoringcontrol} identified a \emph{monitoring-control gap}: a model may detect misinformation in its context yet still let it influence its final answer. Detecting poisoned evidence therefore does not guarantee it is excluded from answer synthesis. The authors establish that this gap exists; we ask whether deliberative reasoning changes it. Rather than proposing a defense, we compare reasoning-disabled and reasoning-enabled configurations of the same models.

This motivates our metrics. \emph{Cordon Rate} measures the probability that a model detects poisoned evidence and nevertheless produces a poison-aligned answer, conditioned on the no-RAG response not being poison-aligned. \emph{Leakage Rate} measures the probability that poisoned context influences the answer despite an explicit instruction to ignore retrieved documents. \textbf{Under the expected behavior, detected misinformation should not influence the final answer, and explicitly ignored context should have no influence, making \(CR=0\) and \(LR=0\).}

\subsection{Cordon Rate}
The \textit{Cordon Rate} (\(CR\)) measures the probability that a language model both detects a poisoned document and nevertheless produces a poison-aligned answer, conditioned on the no-RAG response not being poison-aligned. To distinguish influence associated with the retrieved poison from a pre-existing response of the model, we exclude cases in which the model produces a poison-aligned answer without retrieved context.

For each test instance, we provide \(M_{\text{RAG}}\) with retrieved contexts containing a poisoned document, generated as described in Appendix~\ref{app:a}. We prompt it to reason step-by-step, check the context for misinformation, and produce a final answer (Appendix~\ref{app:b1}). We then prompt the same model without retrieved context, denoted \(M_{\text{no-RAG}}\), to answer the same question using its general knowledge (Appendix~\ref{app:b2}). A judge model determines whether \(M_{\text{RAG}}\)'s answer is aligned with the poison, whether the model detected the misinformation, and whether \(M_{\text{no-RAG}}\)'s answer is poison-aligned (Appendices~\ref{app:b4} and~\ref{app:b5}). Cases in which \(M_{\text{no-RAG}}\) is already poison-aligned are excluded, since a poison-aligned response cannot then be attributed specifically to the retrieved document.

The Cordon Rate is defined as:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cordon_rate}
\begin{aligned}
CR = P\big(&\text{poison-aligned}(M_{\text{RAG}}) \\
& \land \text{detected}(M_{\text{RAG}}) \mid \\
&\neg\,\text{poison-aligned}(M_{\text{no-RAG}})\big)
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

Empirically, we compute this probability as the proportion of instances in which \(M_{\text{RAG}}\) detects the poison and produces a poison-aligned answer among instances where \(M_{\text{no-RAG}}\) is not poison-aligned. Thus, CR captures the joint occurrence of poison detection and downstream poison influence within the relevant conditioning population.


\subsection{Leakage Rate}
The \textit{Leakage Rate} (\(LR\)) measures the probability that a language model is influenced by a poisoned document despite being explicitly instructed to ignore all retrieved context. This captures implicit susceptibility that persists even when top-down instructions direct the model to rely solely on its parametric knowledge.

For each test instance, we evaluate the same language model \(M\) under two conditions. In the first condition, denoted \(M_{\text{ignore}}\), we provide the full set of retrieved documents including the injected poison, but explicitly instruct the model to ignore all provided documents and rely solely on its parametric knowledge. The prompt is available in Appendix \ref{app:b6}. In the second condition, denoted \(M_{\text{no-RAG}}\), we provide no retrieved context and ask the model to answer based solely on its knowledge. The prompt is available in Appendix \ref{app:b2}.

The judge model \(J\) then determines whether each answer is aligned with the poison. The judge prompt is available in Appendix \ref{app:b4}. An instance is counted if \(M_{\text{ignore}}\) produces a poison-aligned answer while \(M_{\text{no-RAG}}\) does not, indicating that the poison-aligned response is attributable to the retrieved context rather than the model's prior knowledge. The Leakage Rate is defined as:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:leakage_rate}
\begin{aligned}
LR = P\big(&\text{poison-aligned}(M_{\text{ignore}})\ \land \\
&\neg\,\text{poison-aligned}(M_{\text{no-RAG}})\big)
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

Empirically, we compute this probability as the proportion of instances satisfying both conditions among all evaluated instances.

\section{Experiments}

We investigate two research questions:

\textbf{RQ1:} Does enabling deliberative reasoning reduce the influence of poisoned context in RAG systems?

\textbf{RQ2:} Does a language model's ability to detect poisoned evidence correspond to its ability to resist its influence on the final answer?


\subsection{Experimental Setup}
We randomly selected 200 SciFact questions~\cite{Wadden2020Scifact} from BEIR~\cite{Thakur2021Beir} (seed 38) and evaluated DeepSeek-V4-Flash and Qwen3.6-Plus with reasoning disabled and enabled, plus Claude Haiku 4.5 and Sonnet 4.6 for an exploratory cross-model comparison. We believe that SciFact suits studying the monitoring-control gap because scientific claims often require reasoning beyond memorized facts, creating stronger opportunities for contextual influence. See Appendix~\ref{app:reproducibility} for details needed to reproduce the results.

We additionally evaluated the first 40 queries from FiQA~\cite{Maia2018Fiqa} and MS MARCO~\cite{Bajaj2016Msmarco} as an exploratory cross-dataset analysis (Appendix~\ref{app:cross_dataset}). Gemini 2.5 Pro~\cite{comanici2025gemini} served as the automated judge with simplified criteria (Appendix~\ref{app:c}); we manually checked about half of the experiments and found no labeling errors. Poisoned passages were generated primarily with GPT-5.6~\cite{openai2026gpt56systemcard}, using Grok 4.6~\cite{xai2026grok46api} when GPT-5.6 refused.

\subsection{RQ1}
\label{rq1}

Table~\ref{tab:rq1} compares Leakage Rate (LR) and Cordon Rate (CR)
between reasoning-disabled and reasoning-enabled configurations. Values
are reported as proportions with 95\% confidence intervals. For
DeepSeek-V4-Flash, reasoning reduces LR from 0.235 to 0.140 (40\%
relative reduction) and CR from 0.205 to 0.075 (63\% relative
reduction). For Qwen3.6-Plus, LR decreases from 0.240 to 0.170 (29\%
relative reduction), while CR decreases from 0.049 to 0.006 (88\%
relative reduction). The corresponding numbers of Cordon cases are
30/146 versus 12/161 for DeepSeek-V4-Flash and 8/163 versus 1/172 for
Qwen3.6-Plus.

Because the same questions are evaluated under both configurations, we
use two-sided exact McNemar tests to assess whether the paired changes
are systematic. For DeepSeek-V4-Flash, both the LR reduction
($p=0.0066$) and CR reduction ($p=0.0009$) are statistically significant.
For Qwen3.6-Plus, the LR reduction is significant ($p=0.0243$), whereas
the CR comparison does not reach the conventional 0.05 threshold
($p=0.0703$). For CR, the paired test is restricted to the intersection
of questions satisfying the no-RAG eligibility criterion under both
configurations; full paired outcome counts and test results are reported
in Appendix~\ref{app:mcnemar}.

The two metrics capture complementary forms of contextual influence.
LR measures poison influence when the model is explicitly instructed to
ignore retrieved documents, whereas CR measures poison-aligned answering
when the model has detected the poisoned evidence. Thus, reductions in
both metrics indicate lower contextual influence under two distinct
conditions of evidence handling. The LR reduction is statistically
supported for both models, while the CR reduction is strongly supported
for DeepSeek-V4-Flash and shows a similar but statistically weaker
pattern for Qwen3.6-Plus.

Overall, the results provide evidence that reasoning is associated with
lower contextual influence from poisoned evidence, although the
magnitude and statistical evidence for the reduction vary across models
and metrics. An exploratory cross-model comparison with Claude Haiku 4.5
and Sonnet 4.6 is reported in Appendix~\ref{app:crossmodel}.

\subsection{RQ2}
RQ2 asks whether poison detection tracks resistance to poisoned context. We treat detection and downstream influence as distinct dimensions. Table~\ref{tab:rq2} reports Attack Success Rate (ASR) and Poison Detection Rate (PDR) with 95\% Wilson confidence intervals; LR and CR are reported in Table~\ref{tab:rq1}.

Enabling reasoning increases ASR for DeepSeek-V4-Flash from 0.233 to 0.298 and for Qwen3.6-Plus from 0.133 to 0.278, while PDR decreases from 0.965 to 0.665 and from 0.830 to 0.500, respectively. Paired two-sided exact McNemar tests show that the ASR change is not significant for DeepSeek-V4-Flash ($p=0.6636$) but is significant for Qwen3.6-Plus ($p<0.0001$); the PDR reduction is significant for both models ($p<0.0001$).

These changes are compatible because CR requires both poison detection and a poison-aligned RAG answer, whereas ASR requires only the latter. Thus, ASR can increase while CR decreases if poison-aligned answers increasingly occur without explicit poison detection. Because CR is bounded by the detection rate, part of its reduction may therefore reflect lower detection. Conditioning on detection in Appendix~\ref{app:cond} addresses this directly: the Conditional Cordon Rate (CCR) also decreases under reasoning, from 0.211 to 0.107 for DeepSeek-V4-Flash and from 0.058 to 0.011 for Qwen3.6-Plus, although the latter is based on a single detected case in the reasoning-enabled condition.

Taken together, the results show that detection frequency alone does not characterize downstream resistance. In the reasoning-enabled configuration, models detect poison less often, are less likely to follow it when it is detected, and do not exhibit lower overall attack success.

\section{Conclusion}
We investigate whether deliberative reasoning changes the relationship between poison detection and downstream influence in RAG. Across two models, reasoning lowers Leakage and Cordon Rates despite lower Poison Detection Rate and higher Attack Success Rate, showing that detection and downstream resistance capture distinct aspects of poisoning behavior. In particular, detecting poisoned evidence does not by itself characterize whether it influences the final answer. These results motivate evaluating poison detection together with downstream contextual influence when assessing knowledge-poisoning vulnerability in RAG. Beyond RAG, such effects may arise in multi-agent systems when an agent generates misinformation through hallucination and places it in its own or another agent's context. Even when detected as incorrect, such information may still influence subsequent responses or actions. Future work should investigate how to better separate information detection from answer synthesis and whether this distinction generalizes to broader agentic settings.


\section*{Limitations}
Our evaluation focuses on 200 SciFact questions, supplemented by exploratory
experiments on FiQA and MS MARCO, reflecting the inference costs of evaluating
multiple proprietary models across reasoning configurations. For construct
validity, we use reasoning-enabled and reasoning-disabled configurations as an
operational proxy for deliberative reasoning. This vendor-defined setting may
affect aspects of inference beyond reasoning itself; however, within-model
comparisons provide a controlled basis for examining its relationship with
contextual influence. Our evaluation also relies on automated judging, making
judge-model reliability a consideration for validity. For SciFact,
Appendix~\ref{app:c} describes safeguards that substantially simplify the
judging process and reduce judge-model error. Finally, we use synthetic
poisoned passages generated through a primary poisoning pipeline. Evaluating
additional poisoning strategies and real-world attack settings is an
important direction for future work.

\section{Judge Model Error Reduction}
\label{app:c}
Judge errors can distort the reported metrics, so we simplify each judge task as much as possible. The judge is Gemini 2.5 Pro, separate from all target models.

\textbf{SciFact (main results).} We require the target model to report the snippet number(s) it flags as misinformation when provided with RAG, along with a \texttt{Final Verdict: Yes/No}. Since we know each poison's direction and snippet number, the judge's decisions can be reduced to near-deterministic rules: poison alignment is identified when the RAG verdict aligns with the poison direction; poison detection is identified when the flagged snippet matches the poisoned snippet; and the no-RAG response is separately evaluated for poison alignment. These judgments determine the conditions used in the Cordon Rate. To further validate these labels, we manually checked half of the experiments from each model for poison detection and poison alignment (covering both reasoning configurations). No errors were found in the manually inspected subset.

\textbf{FiQA and MS MARCO (exploratory).} These datasets do not provide a binary verdict, so the above rules do not apply; responses were inspected manually.

The poison incorrectness check (Appendix~\ref{app:a}) is the only less-constrained judge task and is used only during poison construction. Residual judge error remains a limitation.


\section{Resistance Conditioned on Detection}
\label{app:cond}
Because CR combines poison detection with downstream influence, a lower CR can partly reflect a lower detection rate. We therefore also report the Conditional Cordon Rate (CCR), which isolates downstream influence among cases where the poison was detected. Specifically,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ccr}
\begin{aligned}
CCR = P\big(&\text{poison-aligned}(M_{\text{RAG}}) \mid \\
& \text{detected}(M_{\text{RAG}}) \\
& \land\, \neg\,\text{poison-aligned}(M_{\text{no-RAG}})\big) \\
= CR \,/\, P\big(&\text{detected}(M_{\text{RAG}}) \mid \\
& \neg\,\text{poison-aligned}(M_{\text{no-RAG}})\big)
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}


Thus, while CR captures the joint occurrence of poison detection and subsequent poison-aligned influence among eligible questions, CCR measures the proportion of eligible detected cases in which the detected poison nevertheless affects the final RAG answer. Reporting CCR therefore helps distinguish a reduction in CR caused by lower poison detection from a reduction caused by greater resistance after detection.

CCR is lower with reasoning for both models, but the reduction is smaller than for CR for DeepSeek-V4-Flash (49\% vs.\ 63\%), indicating that part of the CR reduction reflects lower poison detection. Because detection differs between configurations, the detected subsets may differ; therefore, we interpret CCR descriptively rather than as a fully controlled comparison.

\section{Exploratory Cross-Dataset Analysis} \label{app:cross_dataset}
To explore whether susceptibility to contextual influence varies across
datasets, we evaluated DeepSeek-V4-Flash with reasoning disabled and enabled
on the first 40 questions from FiQA and MS MARCO. Both configurations
produced zero Leakage and Cordon Rates on all MS MARCO questions and all but
one FiQA question; the single exception had a Leakage Rate of 1.

In contrast, the non-zero rates observed on SciFact indicate that contextual
influence can vary across datasets. These exploratory results suggest that
susceptibility may depend on properties of the questions or tasks, but the
limited sample does not allow us to identify the underlying factors. A
systematic analysis of question difficulty, familiarity, and other task
characteristics is left for future work.

This example illustrates the monitoring--control gap: detection does not guarantee resistance, and the effect can differ across reasoning configurations.
